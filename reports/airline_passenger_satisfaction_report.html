01 / Problem statement

Turn passenger feedback into a focused service strategy.

Airlines compete on more than price and schedule. Cleanliness, comfort, onboard service, digital touchpoints, cabin experience, and operational reliability all shape whether a journey feels worth repeating. This project asks which observed experience factors are most closely associated with overall passenger satisfaction—and whether those patterns persist once passenger and trip context are considered.

Business objective

Identify service attributes and passenger segments with the strongest satisfaction gaps so improvement effort can be directed toward the moments most likely to matter.

Analytical objective

Describe the sample, visualize relationships, and formally test service quality, travel class, and delay hypotheses using effect sizes, adjusted models, and sensitivity checks.

02 / Data overview

A large, analysis-ready survey with transparent limitations.

What the data contains

Passenger age and recorded gender; first-time or returning status; business or personal travel; cabin class; flight distance and delays; fourteen service ratings; and a binary satisfaction outcome.

City, device, fare, route, aircraft, loyalty tier, delay cause, and repeat-customer history are unavailable.

Quality review

All 129,880 IDs are unique. There are no complete duplicate rows, malformed values, invalid rating ranges, or engineered-feature mismatches. The 393 missing arrival delays were retained and flagged rather than imputed.

Undocumented zero service ratings are preserved in source fields and excluded only in dedicated clean analysis fields.

The outcome combines neutral and dissatisfied passengers into a single category.

Who is represented?

81.7% are returning customers and 69.1% travel for business. Business class represents 47.9%, Economy 44.9%, and Economy Plus 7.2%. Median age is 40 and the recorded gender split is nearly even.

Interpretation guardrail: overall results reflect a sample dominated by returning and business-purpose travelers. Segment composition must be considered before making operational conclusions.
03 / Analysis

The evidence builds across three connected stories.

Service quality

Operational service is rated more strongly than digital touchpoints. More importantly, the three services in the first hypothesis separate satisfied from unsatisfied passengers.

Clean means treat undocumented zero ratings as missing.

Observed rates rise most clearly at ratings 4 and 5; seat comfort is less orderly at ratings 1–3.

Travel class and context

Business class has a large raw advantage, and it remains after adjustment. Yet travel purpose changes the level of satisfaction within every class.

Adjusted probabilities account for age, gender, customer status, travel purpose, flight distance, and departure delay.

Business-purpose travel has a higher baseline across all three classes.

Flight disruption

Satisfaction falls rapidly from no delay to moderate delay. Formal models confirm that the pattern is nonlinear: the steepest decline occurs early, then flattens or partially rebounds at longer durations.

All extreme but plausible delay values were retained. Delay units are not documented in the source.

04 / Hypothesis testing

All three null hypotheses are rejected—effect size gives the result meaning.

H1

Service quality is associated with satisfaction

The joint categorical logistic model for cleanliness, seat comfort, and onboard service was significant. In the adjusted trend model, a one-point increase corresponded with odds ratios of 1.44, 1.32, and 1.82 respectively.

H2

Business class has materially higher satisfaction

Business class leads Economy by 50.7 percentage points and Economy Plus by 44.8 points in the raw data. The global association is substantial (Cramér’s V = 0.503) and remains after adjustment.

H3

Delay is associated with lower satisfaction

Departure and arrival delay are both significant, with nonlinear spline models fitting better than a constant linear effect. At 60 units, adjusted satisfaction is 9.7 points lower for departure delay and 11.6 points lower for arrival delay versus zero.

REJECT H0

Rejecting a null hypothesis indicates evidence of association in this dataset. It does not prove causation.

05 / Key insights

Four ideas explain the passenger experience.

The biggest controllable signal is onboard service.

Among the three core measures, onboard service has the largest adjusted one-point association with satisfaction. It is both a service-design priority and a frontline coaching opportunity.

Digital experience is the clearest quality gap.

Inflight Wi-Fi and online booking hold the two lowest clean mean ratings. Improving them can address visible friction without assuming they alone will change overall satisfaction.

Class is powerful, but context is inseparable.

Business class remains ahead after adjustment, while travel purpose and returning status substantially reshape the raw class comparison. Segment-level action is more useful than a single fleetwide average.

Disruption hurts early and then plateaus.

The delay effect is nonlinear. Preventing small disruptions from becoming moderate ones may offer more leverage than treating every additional unit as equally damaging.

06 / Recommendations

Translate evidence into a focused operating agenda.

1

Prioritize onboard-service consistency

Use flight- and crew-level quality monitoring, targeted coaching, and service-recovery playbooks. Track onboard-service ratings together with satisfaction, not in isolation.

2

Repair the digital journey

Investigate Wi-Fi reliability and online-booking friction with task-level diagnostics. Add device and journey-stage data in future surveys so product teams can locate failure points.

3

Design class-specific improvement plans

Protect the Business-class experience while focusing Economy and Economy Plus work on core comfort and service basics. Separate business-purpose from personal-purpose performance in reporting.

4

Intervene before delays become moderate

Set operational triggers at early delay thresholds, improve proactive communication, and measure whether recovery actions narrow the satisfaction gap among disrupted passengers.

5

Build the data needed for causal and retention decisions

Add stable customer IDs, survey and booking dates, route, fare, loyalty tier, aircraft, delay cause, compensation, city, and device. These fields enable true cohort retention and stronger causal evaluation.

07 / Interpretation notes

What this report can—and cannot—claim.

What is supported

Strong, repeatable associations between satisfaction and service ratings, cabin class, and flight disruption; transparent effect sizes; adjusted comparisons using available covariates; and consistent sensitivity results.

What is not supported

Causal claims, true customer retention, neutral-versus-dissatisfied separation, or conclusions based on unavailable route, fare, device, city, loyalty, aircraft, and disruption-cause information.
